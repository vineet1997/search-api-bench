Exa vs TinyFish

TinyFish gives away web search and page fetching for free. I ran both against my own research stack to see what the zero actually costs. The answer wasn't the one I expected — and the most useful thing I found had nothing to do with price.

Four suites · 3 repeats · deterministic scoring Verdict: route per call, not per vendor

I use Exa for live web calls in a research pipeline that hunts down obscure institutional program pages — fellowships, residencies, visiting-researcher posts. It works, and it costs money. So when I found TinyFish advertising unlimited free search and fetch, the obvious question was whether I could just swap it in.

The short answer is no, but not for the reason I assumed going in. The two products are good at genuinely different jobs, and the interesting result is a routing table, not a winner.

What "free" actually means

TinyFish's Search and Fetch are free at any wallet balance, including zero. No card, no monthly cap. That part is real. The limits are not unlimited, and they have tightened since this run: at the time it was 30 search requests/minute and 150 fetch URLs/minute; as of 2026-11-17 the free tier also caps 500 searches/hour and 1,000 fetch URLs/day. Fine for a paced crawl, fatal for a wide parallel sweep — and a hard ceiling for anything crawling seriously.

What makes the comparison interesting is what Exa charges for. It isn't really the search:

How I set it up

A benchmark you can talk yourself into is worthless, so I built the thing to make that hard. Three constraints did most of the work.

Collection and judgement are separate programs. The runner only writes raw JSONL; the scorer makes zero network calls and contains zero model judgement. Same raw files in, byte-identical scorecard out — I verified it by diffing three consecutive runs. That means I could rewrite the rubric after seeing the data without re-spending a cent, which turned out to matter a lot.

Ground truth for extraction comes from a third party. For the fetch tests I needed to know what a page actually says. Asking either contestant is circular, so I used Firecrawl — a third provider — as referee, and scored each product on how much of the referee's text it reproduced. Neither contestant gets to define what "correct" means.

Each product is scored on its best variant. Where a product had multiple modes, it gets credit for whichever did best. If it loses, it loses on merit.

Four suites, weighted by how often my code actually makes each kind of call — not by how interesting the call is.

S1 · KNOWN-ITEM RETRIEVAL · 25 queries · weight 0.25

Looking up something by name

Twenty-five queries that name a real program — "Recurse Center programmer retreat application" — where exactly one host is the right answer. This is the bread-and-butter keyword sweep.

A dead heat on whether the answer is found, and a small TinyFish win on where it lands. Exa is faster (1.3s vs 3.6s at the median) and needs one call where TinyFish needs two, because TinyFish returns a fixed 10 results per page.

For this job the free option is simply as good. Every dollar I was spending on named lookups was waste.

S2 · SEMANTIC DISCOVERY · 12 queries · weight 0.30

Finding things you can only describe

This is the suite I care about most, and the one with no keyword to lean on. The queries are sentences: "a paid six-month research residency at an AI lab for an experienced software engineer who does not have a PhD." There's no name to match. Exa runs this through its neural mode. TinyFish has no semantic mode at all — its only lever is a purpose field for describing intent.

Exa wins, reaching 78.4% of parity — below my 90% replacement threshold. The gap is real but smaller than "has a neural index" versus "doesn't" would suggest. Keyword search on a well-written descriptive sentence gets you a surprising distance.

One clean negative result: TinyFish's purpose parameter changed the returned URLs in 0 of 36 comparisons. Identical lists, every time. It is not a semantic-search knob, and I'd stop passing it.

Read these absolutes as a floor. 16.7% recall looks terrible for a search engine. It mostly reflects my gold sets being too narrow — Exa returned real AI-safety fellowships like cbai.ai and iliad.ac that I simply hadn't listed. Both engines faced the identical list, so the ratio holds; the absolute numbers understate both.
S3 · LOOKALIKE EXPANSION · 10 seeds · weight 0.15

Growing a list from one good hit

Give it one program's URL, get back more like it. Exa has a dedicated findSimilar endpoint. TinyFish has nothing equivalent, so I built the best surrogate I could — pull the seed page's outbound links, then search on the seed's own title — to measure the gap rather than assume it.

This suite is where I learned the most, because my first version of it was wrong. I'd assumed findSimilar returns competitor organisations. It doesn't — it returns similar pages, and for a homepage seed that means a lot of the seed's own subpages. Scored against my original gold sets, both products got exactly 0.0%. A benchmark where everyone scores zero is measuring the benchmark, not the products.

So I redefined it around what the call is actually for in my pipeline — growing a seed list — and measured new-domain yield instead:

This is the one unambiguous, order-of-magnitude result in the whole exercise: 27× more new domains per seed. If you expand seed lists from URLs, there is no free substitute. Don't try to fake it — I tried, and the fake returns the page you already had.

S4 · CONTENT EXTRACTION · 24 URLs · weight 0.30

Turning a URL into text

Twenty-four URLs chosen to hurt: PDFs, JS-only single-page apps, EU cookie walls, a legacy government site, a soft paywall, a raw JSON endpoint. Scored against the Firecrawl referee.

The thing I actually went looking for money and found instead

While checking why Exa scored 20% on an arXiv abstract page, I found this. It is reproducible across all three repeats.

Correctness finding

Exa attached another paper’s title and date to the right paper

I asked for the RAG paper, arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401. Exa returned the correct paper body — 74.3% of its distinctive sentences, verbatim, with the right title and author line inside the text — underneath a metadata header belonging to a different paper, dated six years later. Byte-identical for the abstract page and the PDF: two different URLs, one response, three times out of three.

Two independent extractors returned correctly-labelled text. Re-checked 2026-11-22: this no longer reproduces — Exa now returns the correct title and identifier, and the metadata-enrichment block the bad header lived in is gone. It was not reported in between, so no credit is claimed for the fix. Scope is narrow — one collision group in twenty-four URLs — and I'm not claiming Exa is broadly unreliable. But the failure mode is the worrying kind.

A failed fetch is loud. You see the error, you retry, you move on. Wrong content arrives looking exactly like right content and flows straight into your corpus as fact. In a pipeline that reads pages to extract eligibility rules and deadlines, that is worse than an outage.

The practical rule I've adopted: if an extraction feeds a citation, a dataset, or a claim I'm going to publish, fetch it twice with different providers and compare. That's cheap now that one of the two providers is free — which is an argument for keeping both integrated that has nothing to do with price.

Where I was wrong

Three metrics changed after I saw data. That's exactly how a benchmark gets quietly rigged, so here they are.

A subdomain bug that reversed a verdict

My matcher required an exact host match. So when Exa found alignment.anthropic.com — the actual Anthropic Fellows page — it scored as a miss against gold anthropic.com. Fixing it to accept subdomains moved S2 from TinyFish winning at 105.5% to Exa winning at 78.4%.

My bug had been hiding a real Exa advantage. If I'd published the first run I'd have had the headline exactly backwards.

A suite that measured a capability neither product claims

The S3 rewrite described above. Both scoring 0.0% was the tell. When every contestant scores zero, suspect the ruler.

An extraction metric that graded typography

I matched reference sentences by exact substring. On PDFs the referee renders an author line as Patrick Lewis††, Ethan Perez* while another extractor emits Patrick Lewis†‡, Ethan Perez⋆. Same text, different glyphs, scored as a total miss. Normalising before comparison raised both products (Exa 38.6→51.4%, TinyFish 44.3→55.7%) and didn't change who won.

There's a fourth I couldn't fix: my gold sets are hand-curated by one person, and S2's low absolutes are the visible symptom. Widening them is the highest-value next improvement, and it's the part I'd most want someone else's eyes on.

That's a better outcome than a clean swap would have been. The two calls TinyFish takes over are the high-volume ones — named lookups and page fetches are most of what my pipeline does — so most of the spend goes to zero. The two Exa keeps are the ones with no substitute at any price, and keeping the integration alive is also the hedge if a free tier stops being free.

If you're evaluating this yourself

Three things I'd do differently, or would tell you to do:

Split collection from scoring. Everything good in this exercise came from being able to re-score without re-spending. I rewrote the rubric three times on data I'd already paid for.

Use a third provider as referee. The moment you ask one contestant what the right answer is, you've lost. Firecrawl cost me a few cents and made the extraction numbers defensible.

Distrust unanimous results. Both suites where the two products scored identically — the 0.0% in S3, the 0% anchor recall on PDFs — were broken measurement, not real ties. Agreement between competitors is a smell.

And one flat-out gotcha that cost me an hour: neither provider follows an apex-to-www redirect. https://jsps.go.jp/ returns nothing from both; https://www.jsps.go.jp/ returns the full page from both. I briefly thought I'd found a TinyFish weakness before checking Exa and finding the identical behaviour. Normalise your URLs.
